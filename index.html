01 / THE CHALLENGE

A map can show where.
It cannot explain what happens there.

PROBLEM STATEMENT

International and transnational visitors must assemble a sacred-site journey from disconnected, multilingual, and unevenly verified sources—often without knowing when a ritual happens, how to participate respectfully, or whether an object or online service is trustworthy.

01BEFORE THE VISIT

Practical information is fragmented.

Opening hours, ritual schedules, transport, entrances, accessibility, and etiquette live across map listings, temple pages, blogs, and social posts.

RESULT · missed rituals, difficult arrivals, uncertain behaviour
02AT THE SITE

Meaning is difficult to access.

A visitor may see offerings, statues, chants, and ritual actions but lack multilingual explanation of their relationships and local significance.

RESULT · observation without cultural understanding
03AROUND SACRED OBJECTS

Education and commerce blur together.

Amulets, candles, and statues circulate through temples, dealers, markets, and social media with inconsistent information about use, maker, consecration, and provenance.

RESULT · confusion, misinformation, and reduced trust
04FROM A DISTANCE

Digital access loses context.

Livestreams and short-form posts extend ritual participation, yet often detach sacred practice from place, history, consent, and community interpretation.

RESULT · access without provenance or accountability
DESIGN FOCUS

How might we connect place, time, ritual, object, media, and community knowledge in one experience—without turning sacred life into either a generic tourist listing or an uncritical marketplace?

02 / THE SOLUTION

One connected system.
Four focused responses.

Sacred City turns scattered information into a contextual journey. It supports planning, on-site understanding, responsible object research, and remote participation through one shared collection.

FROM ANYWHERE

Access authorized livestreams and recorded rituals alongside historical interpretation, community guidance, and object records—rather than encountering sacred practice as decontextualized media.

  • Contextualized livestream access
  • Recorded ritual archive
  • Historical maps and timelines
  • Multilingual educational materials
PLATFORM ROLE

07 / ETHICS AS EXPERIENCE

Visibility is not
the same as permission.

The archive must communicate who can see, record, interpret, and circulate sacred practices.

Community authority

Local contributors can correct, contextualize, or restrict records.

Context before playback

Ritual media opens with etiquette, source, and consent information.

Visible provenance

Every item carries creator, date, source, rights, and uncertainty.

Not everything is a product

Sacred objects are contextualized rather than reduced to shopping links.
